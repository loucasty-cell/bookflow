# Keep the reader calm, spend spectacle elsewhere

Bookflow should not migrate to React Three Fiber, and it should not put 3D, motion or sound behind book text by default. The strongest creative return comes from platform features it already has or gets for free: CSS View Transitions, the CSS Custom Highlight API, scroll-driven animation, `@property`, GSAP plugins that ship inside the existing `gsap` dependency, and plain SVG or Canvas 2D for procedural covers and stats art. R3F 9.8.1 is compatible with Bookflow's React 19.0.0. It earns its cost only as a lazy-loaded island for an opt-in 3D bookshelf or stats "world" on the library and stats surfaces. A minimal R3F scene measured **~308 KB gzip against ~193 KB for React plus a tree-shaken raw three scene**, because R3F registers the whole three namespace. The UX evidence sets the boundary. Decorative, irrelevant material lowers learning from text (**g = -0.33**). Background sound of every kind lowers reading performance slightly but reliably. Screens already cost **g = -0.21** in comprehension against paper. The rule that follows: reader features must be functional or opt-in, decorative features belong on the landing, library and stats surfaces, and every animated feature needs a reduced-motion path, a pause path, and a DOM fallback. The rest of this report says which stacks to use, which to reject, and where each of about twenty concrete features belongs.

## Evidence puts every decorative effect off the reading surface

The case for a calm reader rests on meta-analyses, not taste. Rey's 2012 meta-analysis of "seductive details", meaning interesting but irrelevant additions, found negative effects on retention and transfer. Sundararajan and Adesope's 2020 update put the effect at **g = -0.33** ([Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2019/6/20-1); [ACU Inspire](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details)). An animated rain shader or dust field behind a paragraph is a textbook seductive detail. It also stacks on a known penalty: Delgado et al. pooled 54 studies with more than 170,000 participants and found screen reading trails paper by **g = -0.21**. The gap is larger for expository text and under time pressure, and it **grew between 2000 and 2017** ([Delgado et al. 2018](https://www.uv.es/lasalgon/papers/Delgado%202018%20dont%20throw%20away%20your%20printed%20books.pdf)). Some reviews argue seductive details can be neutral when overall cognitive load is low ([Springer 2023](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11251-023-09632-w)). That is the steelman for ambient visuals on the landing page and in the library, where nobody is trying to comprehend anything. It does not cover the reader body. No controlled study of animated backgrounds behind adult body text was found, so the reader rule extrapolates from this adjacent evidence rather than coming from a direct test.

Sound follows the same pattern. Vasilev, Kirkby and Angele's Bayesian meta-analysis of 65 studies found that noise, speech and music each harm reading by a small but reliable amount. Intelligible speech and lyrics do the most damage ([PMC6139986](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6139986/)). Fast, loud instrumental music disrupts comprehension most among instrumental music ([Thompson et al. 2012](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/ThompsonEtAl2012.pdf)). Introverts appear more affected than extraverts, though the samples are small ([Furnham & Strbac 2002](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/2002-furnham.pdf)). The one pro-sound result is narrow. White or pink noise gave participants with ADHD symptoms a **small benefit (g = 0.249)** across 13 studies and 335 people. No brown-noise studies existed, and the same noise may distract everyone else ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/white-and-pink-noise-show-promise-in-enhancing-attention-in-those-with-adhd/); [OHSU](https://ohsu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-do-white-noise-or-pink-noise-)). This supports an opt-in pink-noise bed with no efficacy claims, and nothing more ambitious.

Page-turn animation is a preference feature, not a comprehension feature. A study of 46 fourth graders found no comprehension difference between scrolling and page-by-page display ([TOJET](https://www.tojet.net/abstracts/v10i3/10311_abstract.htm)). The most useful product signal is Apple's. Apple Books dropped the realistic curl for a slide in iOS 16.0, then restored it in 16.4 as one of three options: "Curl", "Slide" (the default) and "None" ([iThinkDifferent](https://www.ithinkdiff.com/apple-books-traditional-page-turn-ios-16-4/)). Readers wanted the curl available, not imposed.

Gamification evidence backs Bookflow's existing rule of deterministic progress with opt-in rewards. Sailer and Homner's meta-analysis found small positive effects on cognition (**g = .49**), motivation (**.36**) and behavior (**.25**), but the motivational and behavioral effects were unstable in rigorous studies ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)). The finding that expected tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation is real but contested ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2001_DeciKoestnerRyan.pdf); [Cameron, Banko & Pierce](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF03392017)). Evidence of "streak anxiety" is qualitative only, but it all points the same way ([UKWMS](https://repositori.ukwms.ac.id/47113/6/BAB%205.pdf)). Achievement reveals therefore belong after a session ends, on the library or stats surface, with no-loss streak framing.

Accessibility turns these choices into requirements. SC 2.3.3 (AAA) asks that non-essential motion triggered by interaction can be disabled. Its sufficient techniques are honoring `prefers-reduced-motion` in CSS (C39) or JavaScript (SCR40), or offering an in-app control ([W3C Understanding 2.3.3](https://w3c.github.io/wcag/understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)). Auto-playing motion falls under SC 2.2.2 (Level A), which requires a pause mechanism for motion that runs longer than five seconds. Vestibular dysfunction affected **35.4% of US adults aged 40 and over** in NHANES data ([PubMed 19468085](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19468085)). That is a balance measure, not a measure of motion sensitivity on screens, but it explains why camera moves and parallax count as risk. One related caution: OpenDyslexic showed no readability benefit in eye-tracking or in reading-rate studies ([PMC5629233](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5629233/)). Typeface options should stay as preferences, never as claims that they improve comprehension.

## Raw three stays; R3F enters only as a lazy library island

The current ambient layer already does what an R3F rewrite would promise. `src/shared/components/AmbientDustCanvas.jsx` uses a raw `WebGLRenderer`. It picks a still frame when `prefers-reduced-motion` is set or the quality tier is STATIC, pauses on `visibilitychange` and IntersectionObserver, disposes resources on unmount, and loads through `React.lazy` from the landing page only. These facts come from the repo reads recorded in the notes; they were not re-verified while writing this report. A port would buy declarative syntax and cost bytes. R3F's `<Canvas>` runs `extend(THREE)` on the full three namespace, which defeats tree-shaking. The measured esbuild totals are below. They are esbuild figures, not Vite output, and Bookflow's real chunk size was not measured ([R3F canvas docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pmndrs/react-three-fiber/master/docs/API/canvas.mdx); package metadata from the [npm registry](https://registry.npmjs.org/@react-three/fiber)).

| Stack (minimal points scene) | gzip | Verdict for Bookflow |
|---|---|---|
| React DOM + raw three, tree-shaken | 192.9 KB | Current approach; keep |
| React DOM + R3F 9.8.1 `frameloop="demand"` | 308.3 KB | Only for a lazy 3D library/stats island |
| + drei PerformanceMonitor, AdaptiveDpr, Sparkles | 310.7 KB | drei helpers are nearly free once R3F is in |
| + @react-three/postprocessing Bloom, Vignette | 334.5 KB | Avoid; also caps three below r187 |
| `three/webgpu` + TSL minimal | 245.1 KB | Not justified for calm effects |
| OGL points | 13.4 KB | Candidate if the dust layer is ever re-platformed |
| regl / PixiJS 8 | 41.2 / 152.6 KB | Neither fits |

Compatibility is not the blocker. R3F 9.8.1 (published 2026-09-24) declares React `>=19 <19.4` and three `>=0.156`, so it fits Bookflow's React 19.0.0 and three ^0.186 ([npm registry](https://registry.npmjs.org/@react-three/fiber)). Recent releases added React 19.3 support, Strict Mode fixes, renderer disposal on unmount, and stable uniform references for pierce props ([R3F releases](https://github.com/pmndrs/react-three-fiber/releases)). Upkeep is the real cost. R3F bundles its own reconciler, so each React minor needs a matching R3F release. `postprocessing` 6.39.5 caps three at `<0.187.0`. R3F v10 is a WebGPU-first alpha that renames `state.gl` to `state.renderer` ([R3F releases](https://github.com/pmndrs/react-three-fiber/releases)). Two pmndrs packages look attractive but are effectively unmaintained. `@react-three/a11y` was last published in **May 2022** and `@react-three/offscreen` in **May 2023**; both declare only R3F `>=8` ([npm a11y](https://registry.npmjs.org/@react-three/a11y); [npm offscreen](https://registry.npmjs.org/@react-three/offscreen)). Bookflow should build accessibility as DOM overlays rather than depend on either.

WebGPU is also not worth adopting yet. It is on by default in Chrome desktop, Safari 26 and Firefox on Windows and macOS, but Firefox on Linux and Android and some Android GPUs still lack it ([gpuweb wiki](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/wiki/Implementation-Status)). `WebGPURenderer` falls back to WebGL 2, so a later migration is cheap. A dust or grain effect gains nothing from compute shaders, while the minimal bundle grows by about 113 KB gzip.

If Bookflow does build a WebGL bookshelf or constellation, the adoption path is fixed. Leave `AmbientDustCanvas.jsx` unchanged. Put the new scene in its own feature folder behind `React.lazy`, and configure Vite chunking so only one copy of three loads. Use `frameloop="demand"` so the GPU idles; R3F's docs name the default 60 fps loop as what "drains batteries the most" ([R3F scaling performance](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pmndrs/react-three-fiber/master/docs/advanced/scaling-performance.mdx)). Clamp `dpr` to `[1, 1.5]`. Use drei's `PerformanceMonitor` with `onFallback` to drop to a static image, and wrap the canvas in `aria-hidden` with real DOM buttons on top. AGENTS.md requires explicit user approval before adding `@react-three/fiber` or `@react-three/drei`.

## Browser features and GSAP plugins deliver most of the delight for free

The cheapest creative features need no WebGL at all. Same-document View Transitions now ship in **Chrome 111, Firefox 144 and Safari 18**, and transition `types` (for choosing forward or back animations) in Chrome 125, Firefox 147 and Safari 18.2 ([MDN browser-compat-data 8.1.5](https://registry.npmjs.org/@mdn/browser-compat-data)). For a single-page app like Bookflow, that makes a shared-element "book lifts off the shelf" transition from the library to the reader a zero-dependency feature. The fallback is an instant swap whenever reduced motion is set.

For reader typography the key primitive is the **CSS Custom Highlight API**. It styles arbitrary `Range`s without wrapping text in spans ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/CSS_custom_highlight_API)). It is supported in Chrome 105, Firefox 140 and Safari 17.2; Firefox's `::highlight()` selector arrived in 149 ([BCD](https://registry.npmjs.org/@mdn/browser-compat-data)). This is the one technique that adds visual craft inside the book text while respecting both Bookflow invariants: text stays React text nodes, and selection and screen-reader flow are untouched. Named highlights for notes, search hits and the current read-aloud word all work this way. Styling is limited to roughly the `::selection` property set ([LogRocket](https://blog.logrocket.com/getting-started-css-custom-highlight-api)). No source confirmed that transitions animate on `::highlight()`, so animated ink is untested. GSAP SplitText and splitting.js insert spans into the DOM, so they must stay on decorative strings React does not reconcile, such as chapter title cards and badge labels.

Scroll-driven animation (`animation-timeline`) ships in Chrome 115 and Safari 26 but not in stable Firefox ([BCD](https://registry.npmjs.org/@mdn/browser-compat-data)). It suits a chapter progress hairline or a gentle fade near the 0.38 focus rail, as progressive enhancement only, while the JavaScript focus rail remains the source of truth. `@property` (baseline since Firefox 128) enables an animated `--progress` ring and smooth color transitions on the active sentence. `text-wrap: balance` is baseline for headings. `text-wrap: pretty` works in Chrome and Safari 26 but not Firefox, and its cost on long chapters was never measured. The CSS Paint API (Houdini) is Chromium-only and should be skipped.

GSAP is the main creative engine already in the dependency list (`package.json:22`, `gsap ^3.15.0`). Its formerly paid plugins are now free under the no-charge Standard license ([GSAP Standard License](https://gsap.com/community/standard-license/); [npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/gsap)). Measured gzip costs are small: **SplitText 3.7 KB, DrawSVG 2.2 KB, MorphSVG 9.6 KB** on top of a 28.3 KB core. An emblem "inked" with DrawSVG when an achievement unlocks, or a bookmark icon morphing from outline to filled, costs a few KB and no new dependency. Rive (**~973 KB gzip** for `rive.js` plus WASM) and dotLottie (**~529 KB gzip**) are far heavier for a handful of badges ([npm Rive](https://registry.npmjs.org/@rive-app/canvas); [npm dotLottie](https://registry.npmjs.org/@lottiefiles/dotlottie-web)). Rive's vendor claim that .riv files are 10-15 times smaller than Lottie JSON ([Rive blog](https://rive.app/blog/rive-as-a-lottie-alternative)) does not offset a runtime of about 1 MB. p5 (284 KB gzip) and PixiJS (237 KB gzip full build) are unjustified for incidental UI ([npm p5](https://registry.npmjs.org/p5); [npm pixi.js](https://registry.npmjs.org/pixi.js)).

Procedural covers and stats art follow the same logic. Generative covers built from metadata have a long open lineage, starting with NYPL Labs' 2014 generator, which seeded colors from title and author length ([NYPL](https://www.nypl.org/blog/2014/09/03/generative-ebook-covers); [variablestudio](https://github.com/variablestudio/var-17006-generative-covers)). Hashing title and author into a seed is a few lines of code, needs no network, and solves a real gap: a local-first app has no cover art. For stats, full d3 is 92 KB gzip ([npm d3](https://registry.npmjs.org/d3)). React-rendered SVG with d3 sub-modules, or visx 4.0 ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/@visx/visx)), keeps the cost small. Competitors show the bar. StoryGraph leads on charts, streaks and wrap-ups, and Fable on streaks and Wrapped-style recaps ([Bookends](https://bookends.app/blog/best-reading-apps-2025/); [Tales of Belle](https://talesofbelle.com/2025/09/11/goodreads-vs-storygraph-vs-fable/)). No major reading app was found shipping 3D stats. That leaves room for Bookflow to stand out, with no data showing users want it.

## Twenty features mapped to surface, default and cost

The table assigns each feature a surface, a default and a cost. "Opt-in" means off until the reader enables it in settings. Costs are gzip and come from the measured figures above; "0" means a browser API or an existing dependency. Every animated row inherits the same baseline: reduced motion (OS query plus an in-app motion toggle) gives a static result, no flashing faster than three times per second, a pause path for anything that loops longer than five seconds, and `aria-hidden` on decorative canvases with real DOM controls.

Two rows need more detail. The bookshelf comes in two forms because elishacoad's comparison of the same shelf in both technologies is decisive. CSS 3D adds about 0 bytes and keeps spines live, focusable and selectable. The WebGL version costs **+136 KB gzip** for three, renders spines as canvas textures that cannot take focus, and needs a working WebGL context, which not every visitor has ([GitHub](https://github.com/elishacoad/3d-bookshelf)). Bookflow already pays for three, so for a WebGL shelf the real cost is R3F plus scene code. The CSS shelf should still be the first optional library view.

For the page curl, the reference is shiarauzo/book-flip. It bends subdivided planes with a `uProgress` and `uBend` shader injected through `onBeforeCompile`, renders on demand, honors reduced motion by snapping, and disposes resources on unmount ([GitHub](https://github.com/shiarauzo/book-flip)). Bookflow scrolls rather than paginates, so the fitting form is a one-pass curl between two snapshots, adapting HP's BSD-3 InvertedPageCurl ([source](https://docs.rs/crate/isf/0.1.0/source/test_files/Inverted%20Page%20Curl.fs)). It plays only at chapter boundaries, and the reading surface is always DOM text.

## Sound, voice and haptics stay opt-in and dependency-free

Audio is cheap in bytes and costly in attention, so every audio feature starts off. It starts only after a user gesture and ships with a volume control. Raw Web Audio with an `AudioWorklet` noise generator covers a pink- or brown-noise bed and soft chapter-turn foley. `AudioWorklet` is supported in Chrome 66, Firefox 76 and Safari 14.1 ([BCD](https://registry.npmjs.org/@mdn/browser-compat-data)). If sample sprites are needed, Howler's core is **8 KB gzip** but has had no release since 2023 ([npm howler](https://registry.npmjs.org/howler)). Tone.js (**79 KB gzip**, stable release from April 2025) only makes sense for genuinely musical scheduling ([npm tone](https://registry.npmjs.org/tone)). The settings copy should offer pink noise as a preference, never as a focus aid, because the benefit is confined to people with ADHD symptoms.

Read-aloud is the most useful audio feature. It is also a privacy hazard. `speechSynthesis` is supported everywhere (Chrome 33, Firefox 49, Safari 7), and sentence-boundary events could drive both the focus rail and the highlight of the current word ([BCD](https://registry.npmjs.org/@mdn/browser-compat-data)). Some platform voices are cloud-backed, though. Filtering to `SpeechSynthesisVoice.localService === true` is the intended guard, but its behavior on each platform is **unverified** and needs device testing before the feature ships under Bookflow's local-first invariant. Fully on-device neural voices are possible. kokoro-js loads Kokoro-82M with q4 to fp32 variants, but reported downloads range from **86 MB to over 300 MB**, and WebGPU mode is reported as Chromium-only ([npm kokoro-js](https://npmjs.com/package/kokoro-js); [OfflineTTS](https://offlinetts.com/blog/kokoro-tts-complete-guide/)). That justifies an explicitly downloaded voice pack at most, never a default.

Haptics need a fix before any new feature. `src/shared/lib/haptics.js` calls `navigator.vibrate` but is not gated on reduced motion, as its own TODO notes. `navigator.vibrate` does not exist on Safari or iOS at all ([BCD](https://registry.npmjs.org/@mdn/browser-compat-data)). The iOS hidden-switch workaround used by web-haptics relies on undocumented behavior, and one single source reports it **patched in iOS 26.5** ([npm web-haptics](https://registry.npmjs.org/web-haptics); [rapidtoolset](https://rapidtoolset.com/en/tool/ios-haptics-tester)). The workable scope is short, gated, Android-first ticks for a bookmark, a goal reached, or a paragraph pin. Device-motion parallax needs an iOS permission prompt and adds vestibular risk, so it should not ship.

## Conclusion

The research flips the premise. The question for Bookflow is not which 3D stack to adopt but where spectacle is allowed. Answering that settles the stack: browser features and the existing GSAP and three dependencies cover nearly every high-value idea, and R3F becomes a bounded, approvable bet for one opt-in library or stats scene. Bookflow's most distinctive creative asset comes out of its constraints. With no cover fetches and only metadata stats, it has to generate its covers, fingerprints and shelves locally and deterministically. That gives it an aesthetic identity competitors relying on publisher art do not have, and it costs almost nothing.

Several open items should be settled before building. First, measure the actual Vite three chunk with `npm run build`, because every R3F cost estimate here comes from esbuild. Second, confirm on real devices how `localService` voices behave before shipping read-aloud. Third, test whether `::highlight()` color transitions animate. Fourth, profile any continuous shader on a mid-range Android phone, since no battery measurements exist. Finally, run a short preference study with users. The biggest gap in this research is the absence of direct evidence on what readers of a calm, local-first app praise or dismiss as gimmicks. Apple's curl reversal suggests the safest pattern is to offer every flourish as a choice with a clear "None" option.
